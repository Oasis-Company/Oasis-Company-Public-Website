HAA is absurd

haa is absurd

This title is clickbait. So let me say it outright: HAA is already the best “school” in the world. You could say HAA is closer to the school in my dreams than any school — except the one I want to found myself, OITS.

The word “absurd” here is not a rejection of HAA's positioning. It is a doubt about the rules of action HAA worships.

HAA's influence is bound to be enormous. It has an extraordinary cross-domain pull: start a company, fail fast, then succeed, become a polymath — it sounds sexy. In Silicon Valley their words are treated as gospel by almost everyone. That is exactly why I wrote this piece.

But before I argue about HAA properly, let me tell my own story first.

You could say I've been soaked in the Silicon Valley narrative since I was six or seven: the stories of the PayPal mafia, Anderson's Makers, and a few other books. And names I had heard so many times I could recite them.

(I don't quite agree with some of Andreessen's views these days — probably because of his ties to right-wing techno-politics.)

It also included the pitch scripts investors have been repeating for decades, and the FOMO tricks of the hunting-investors game.

(Harvard dorms. Silicon Valley garages. And the other trigger-like slices of scene, hitting my mind at all hours.)

From birth until sixteen I lived in a small, remote city in China. Maybe that is why I was so obsessed with these books — those Silicon Valley fantasies. They always carried a dreamlike sense of escape from reality. I felt I had fled this dull industrial city and was sitting on some lawn in the Bay Area, friends around me, and when you had an idea, they would build it with you.

I still remember sitting on a small stool in my school dorm's toilet stall a few years ago, going through nearly every famous book about Silicon Valley entrepreneurship.

(Including my childhood obsession with the metaverse. When Zuckerberg renamed Facebook to Meta, I thought he had stolen my idea. Then came everything that followed — a thorough, total failure.)

But I kept it as my life's goal. A sense of mission filled my body, so for these past few years a large share of the weight of every decision I made came from that part of me. It gave me a constant unreal feeling, like a dream that never ends.

I went through a period (around fifth or sixth grade) when I thought university was meaningless — that any deliberate act of catering to a rule system was a waste of time. I desperately wanted to fly to Silicon Valley, Boston, or Bee Cave in Austin, immediately found Oasis Company, and create the next Google, Apple, Amazon.

Even now, university is still meaningless to me. Or rather, it only carries a subjective, non-urgent meaning in certain respects (going there purely to experience life, or to meet interesting people). What follows is the story of how that non-urgent meaning came to exist in my mind.

Note my wording above: “non-urgent.” Which means university does hold importance along some dimension in my mind. What is it?

When I rethought the value of university much later, the most important part was the structural things it can bring. This should not be simply read as “environment.” It is a structural friction between an external environment and an internal way of thinking. Over the past few years I built a self-consistent thinking system, and I started to feel it was nearly perfect. In fact, under this gradually radicalizing personal way of thinking, I did glide smoothly through some environments — while other environments made me extremely uncomfortable.

In eighth grade I made the decision, officially.

For me, MIT was never the spillover of social-status signaling. It was an idealist's Eden. I treated getting into MIT as a byproduct of personal ability and work — an overflow of personal influence under my own system. Often I didn't care whether a decision was rational (more precisely: the most-probable path under the general social evaluation system). I cared about the value it gave me inside, because I never gave MIT a messianic complex.

So when people asked me: what if you don't get in? I didn't know how to answer, because it was not within my range of consideration.

So I think being radical is fine — on one condition: you truly possess a comprehensive and open internal evaluation system.

That is the single biggest thing I overlooked during my quasi-homeschool years.

Also, order matters. The different external environments your cognitive system passes through at different stages are like building a tower floor by floor — or like the order in which ingredients go into the pot.

A person who studies alone for a long time may already have formed a highly efficient self-framework. But when they enter a genuinely diverse environment, they may have to accept evaluation standards they cannot control, and so on...

At that point, the external environment begins to apply pressure to the internal cognitive structure. This radiates into many possibilities.

Could a highly optimized environment also remove certain necessary structural friction? HAA's environment is highly optimized (in fact).

People will put Silicon Valley's founders into one pattern.

More specifically, they assume these founders all share a moonshot-thinking way of doing things: full of drive, impressive ideas, always stepping off the default path...

In reality, the differences between individuals are enormous. The surface already differs; never mind the unprobeable deep internal structure.

Putting someone into the wrong pattern is harder to fix than outright suppression.

A person can have extreme execution ability, learning speed, and expressiveness, yet lack the ability to reflect on the boundaries of their own cognition.

If these abilities are multiplicatively related, the missing term can cap the performance of the entire system. So we need to understand the structural relations between abilities.

This makes me want to ask a question:

Why do we keep decomposing a person's abilities into a series of independently comparable metrics, while ignoring the structural relations between them?

HAA can be the best school in the world — and the educational philosophy it represents can still have serious flaws.

I am extremely arrogant. I do run into things that frustrate me. But if you had lived through my particular path, you would know: you almost never meet a person who frustrates you. You genuinely believe you are the best person in the world. Even when you do meet someone insane-goatly good, your brain will classify them, compress them, into a pattern that diverges enormously from your own path. You will genuinely feel: if there is one person who can single-handedly turn humanity's future toward the optimistic side, that person is you.

A sense of mission occupies a huge weight in my mind.

I always prided myself on my cognitive humility. Until recently, when I began to doubt whether I was actually humble in my cognition. Or rather, it is precisely because of recent events that my doubt of HAA sank to a deeper level.

(Recent events: )

Horowitz Andreessen Academy wants to supply the world with that sample of “high cognition” and “high execution.”

But those people lack their own thinking systems, and structural friction with the outside world.

Knowledge-type things are indeed extremely easy to acquire — even more so in an era where AI can quickly pull in vertical resources.

Horowitz Andreessen Academy looks like a perfect solution for 2016, designed in 2026.

I can compress the central claim of this piece into one sentence:

Success in one domain does not automatically confer wisdom across domains.

Here is an interesting question: between the Partners and the students at HAA, what do they actually think of each other? Put differently: do those Partners genuinely believe these high schoolers will change the future — or is it for their own fame, or many other considerations?

What does a genuinely good situation of teaching and being taught look like? This reminds me of my AP Seminar teacher (I spent twenty days at this school and am about to withdraw). Let's call him Bear. He said he never treated me as a student. He also admitted that while I was at the school every day (in fact, the reason I came to this place was precisely to test the structural gaps in my own way of thinking), I was extremely lonely in my thinking.

Bear kept saying logic is everything, that mathematical analysis is so simple. When he tutored me (I arrived forty days after the term began), he said I was the smoothest person to talk to that he had ever met, because he could throw words like methodology, metacognition, ontology straight at me. I admit it: Bear is the best teacher I have encountered in K-12 so far. I never imagined I could discuss the design-convergence problems of AI with a teacher in a mainland Chinese high school.

But Bear's thinking has its own deep flaw. Or rather, it over-relies on internal reasoning structures. He kept telling me to pay attention to communication with my teammates, and said my ego's impact on the group was bigger than he expected. But in reality, the interpersonal dynamics of our group were so complicated that to this day I cannot determine how much weight some teammates actually give AP Seminar in their minds.

I can also say Bear's information sources were not rich enough. He is a wonderful interlocutor for philosophy and logic, but he too fell into the trap of the system path — he was still flattening every person into a simplified profile. And it was exactly my conversations with Bear, my thinking about him, and my reflection on myself that finally made me decide to write this piece.

Having discussed structural gaps above, I can now raise my biggest question about HAA:

HAA may be taking one historical period's optimal solution and treating it as the optimal solution for the future.

This is not 2016 Silicon Valley. AI's current role in social dynamics is roughly what “data” was in 2016. So the Gold-Coast Silicon Valley narrative still looks useful. But what about five years from now? That is exactly when HAA's students will “make their grand move.”

I believe AI will change humanity's future — but only after many disruptive innovations have appeared. Definitely not by following the current path and then arriving at AGI.

Absolutely not the way today's Silicon Valley vibe founders build their startups.

So: do not build an environment that is too smooth. Let our worldviews be challenged together, all of us.

Whenever the sun goes down, I might be walking alone on the field, thinking, or in 505 talking happily with friends. I am full of passion for what I am building now, Lemma AI. It will become the most advanced AI learning engine inside the observable universe — and not long from now.

I once worshipped every person in Silicon Valley. Now I am gradually disenchanted. Most of them simply rode their parents into an ecological niche that was suitable and shiny for them, while their interior may carry severe structural gaps. At first I longed to become them. Then I envied them. Then I resented them. And now I feel almost nothing toward them — because we were never in the same coordinate system. That does not mean we will not intersect (in fact, the intersection may be huge). It means I know, with total clarity, who I am. I don't know whether moonshot thinking is feasible enough at my current stage. But it always gives me a sense of mission — and that is enough.

We can say Horowitz Andreessen Academy is absurd — at least from the standpoint of long-term learning and adaptation.

I am glad I formed my epistemic humility here (maybe).

So: HAA is an ambitious, good school. But the degree to which it overfits the people it catalyzes may also be among the worst there is.
